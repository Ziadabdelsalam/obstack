# Retention

Your plan's `retention_days` is what obstack keeps. A sweep runs **every 24
hours**, reads each workspace's current plan from Postgres, and deletes that
workspace's rows past the cutoff from spans, logs, trace summaries, the
metrics rollups and the series catalog, and from the alert events obstack
raised for it and the change events its systems posted.

Behind it, the ClickHouse tables carry a **90-day TTL** as an outer bound. That
ordering matters: a sweep that stops running degrades to keeping telemetry
*longer* than the plan says — the benign direction — never to unbounded growth.
The two Postgres tables — alert events and change events — have no outer bound
of their own: for them, a stopped sweep keeps rows until it runs again.

## "Deleted" means two different things at two different times

Both halves, because only stating one of them would be misleading:

- **Gone from every product read the moment the statement returns.** The delete
  masks the rows immediately, so nothing in the product can see them from that
  instant on.
- **Disk shrinks in the background.** Bytes are reclaimed when the storage engine
  merges the affected parts, which is later and is not something the sweep waits
  for.

## The current plan decides, not the plan at write time

The sweep reads the plan at sweep time. An upgrade therefore **extends** the
retention of rows that were already written, and a downgrade **shortens** it: a
row's lifetime is a function of today's plan. A workspace with no plan row is on
free.

## A trace that straddles the cutoff

Trace summaries expire on the last date the trace was seen, while spans expire on
their own timestamps. **A trace straddling the cutoff loses its older spans while
its summary survives to its own expiry.** The trace still lists; opening it shows
the spans that remain.

## The bound, stated rather than engineered around

One workspace-table delete in ClickHouse gets a bounded statement timeout. At
the measured rate — about 8.4 seconds per million rows on the widest table —
that is on the order of a hundred million past-cutoff rows in a single
workspace before a statement cannot finish. The Postgres tables are swept in
batches of 5,000 rows under a 30-second timeout, repeated until a batch comes
back short.

Past that point the ClickHouse sweep does **not** chunk and does **not** fall
back to a heavyweight mutation. It fails that statement **loudly, counts it, moves on to
the next workspace, and retries the whole thing tomorrow** — the log line names
the workspace and the table.

The consequence is bounded and benign: **that workspace over-retains — in
ClickHouse until the 90-day table TTL takes the rows, in Postgres until the next
sweep succeeds.** Retention is late, never absent, and never at the cost of the
sweep for every other workspace.

## What is not swept

Incidents you write are kept until you delete them. The sweep never touches
them: a postmortem a person typed is not telemetry, and unlike an alert rule or
an SLO it is not a definition the product could re-derive — its title, summary
and impact are the only copy there is. The evidence an incident's timeline
stitches — alert events, change events, error traces — still ages out on your
plan's window, and the incident page says so when it does.

## Operational notes

Every ingest replica runs the sweep. The deletes are idempotent by construction —
two replicas masking the same rows converge on the same state — so there is no
leader, no lease and no jitter to configure.

The sweep deletes telemetry rows and never workspaces. Deleting a workspace is a
separate thing this sweep does not do.
